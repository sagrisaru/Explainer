Light Reading

Understanding what photos of space are showing us

Saskia Grisaru

This was the first image taken by the Hubble Space Telescope shortly after its launch in 1990. Broadcast on BBC, it reached millions. Joe DePasquale, then about 10 years old, was among them.

NASA recognized the value in releasing images from the publicly-funded telescope to the public. Over the following months and years, they published more photos, of better quality (thanks to a manufacturing fix). These photos filled DePasquale with curiosity and awe.

Three decades later, DePasquale would himself have a hand in the first images from Hubble’s successor: the James Webb Space Telescope, launched in 2021. A visual developer, DePasquale works at the Space Telescope Science Institute in Baltimore.

Fundamentally, images of space are scientific documents of “paramount importance,” said Lukas Furtak, who researches galaxy clusters at the University of Texas at Austin. The only way we can observe the universe is through light, and through images we capture that light, Furtak explained.

But this scientific perspective is fundamentally indissociable from a non-scientific one. Images of space are the public face of astronomy, a means for humans to connect to and marvel at the beauty of the cosmos.

For non-astronomers, understanding the images themselves might seem astronomically difficult. And indeed, “in order to really, really, really understand what you’re looking at, you kind of need a PhD,” joked Liz Tarantino, a colleague of DePasquale’s at Space Telescope.

Still, there are ways to make these images more legible to the rest of us. By learning to recognize and distinguish patterns, non-scientists can develop a vocabulary to "read" them. The first step, Tarantino said, is reframing your thinking.

TO UNDERSTAND SPACE PHOTOS, REFRAME YOUR THINKING

Our eyeballs are not 21 feet wide, capable of seeing infrared light, or parked out in space 1 million miles away from Earth. In other words, we cannot see what James Webb Space Telescope sees.

Tens of scientists and visual developers work to translate the data that Webb receives—photons of light captured by cameras—into colors that we can see. “The colors are a translation, like translating a language into something we can understand with our perception,” said Klaus Pontoppidan, a senior research scientist at NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory.

Webb was designed to capture light in the infrared range. Humans can’t see infrared light—we see what are called “visible” wavelengths. Webb’s ability to capture images in the infrared allows it to see through dust, which is ubiquitous in space, said Adam Smercina, an astronomer at Tufts University specialized in nearby galaxies.

Infrared telescopes also allow us to see farther back in time and space than we ever have. Objects farther away in the universe undergo a process called “redshifting,” by which the waves of light they emit are stretched, pulled into the infrared range, as the universe expands. Infrared telescopes enable us to see redshifted objects from the early universe, Furtak said.

Webb is the largest and highest-resolution infrared telescope. Every telescope functions differently from the others, so it’s hard to compare images between them. Webb's images can be distinguished by a characteristic 6-pronged star, which denotes how its mirror diffracts light and is like a “visual signature,” said Alyssa Pagan, another science visual developer working at Space Telescope.

Objects in space leave their own signatures. Our eyes are very good at picking out these patterns, like the difference between a star and more nebulous gas and dust, Smercina said. The universe is “very fractal,” DePasquale said, meaning that the patterns replicate themselves across different scales. From images of protostars measuring less than a single light year across, to images of galaxy clusters several millions of light years wide, these patterns dis- and reassemble, in tune with physical processes at play everywhere in the universe.

PROTOSTARS

Protostars are newborn stars at the very earliest stage of birth. A star is born when clouds of hot gas coalesce, and afterwards, it spends the rest of its lifetime releasing gas and dust back into space. Protostars are still heavily enshrouded in their natal nebulae, said Kevin Luhman, a specialist in brown dwarfs at Pennsylvania State University.

Dust, which is cool, tends to emit light at long, low-energy infrared wavelengths. Because cooler light is redder in the visible spectrum—a redder flame is cooler than a bluer one—scientists assign red tones to cooler light in infrared, too, Luhman explained. When protostars like the one circled here shine through the dust, they appear redder, just as the sun appears red when there’s smoke in the air.

Conversely, bluer nebulae have shorter wavelengths and higher energy, meaning that they correspond to gas that is either hotter than the dust, or closer to us, or both.

PLANETARY NEBULAE

Planetary nebulae are regions of gas and dust sloughed off by a dying star. Photos of them showcase the gas and dust radiating outwards.

This image shows a close-up of the gas and dust from the Helix Nebula, formed into knots and pushed away from the center by stellar winds emanating from the dying star. Hot, blue stars shine in the foreground.

STAR-FORMING REGIONS

Images of star-forming regions capture the dense, cold clouds of dust and gas within which stars form. They feature stars, gas and dust interacting.

Sagittarius B2, pictured here, is a star-forming region in our own galaxy. The pockets of reddish gas are hydrogen, said Adam Ginsburg, who researches cluster formation at the University of Florida. Hydrogen glows when heat from nearby massive stars cause it to ionize, losing an electron and emitting light.

GALAXIES

Galaxies are massive systems made up of stars, gas, dust and dark matter all bound together by gravity. The coalescence of all that matter typically flattens out into a disk, conserving angular momentum. Large galaxies like this one, Centaurus A, have a supermassive black hole at their center, invisible but for the effect that it has on its surroundings, explained Macarena Garcia Marin, a Webb project scientist for Space Telescope and the European Space Agency.

The definition of a galaxy is a little controversial, said Tarantino, because scientists don’t agree on a minimum size. This galaxy is a dwarf galaxy, which tend to have “irregular, clumpy, weird-looking morphologies,” she said.

M82 is more typical in shape, with a dense starry and dusty core and stars spiraling outwards. The prominent outflow of gas and dust from either side indicate intense star formation in the center of the galaxy, Smercina said. The outer, redder dust is made up of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, or PAHs, which are also produced in smoke on Earth. They’re probably the reason that space smells like gunpowder and burnt steak.

GALAXY CLUSTERS AND DEEP FIELDS

Galaxy clusters are groups of hundreds to thousands of individual galaxies held together through gravitational attraction. The deepest images that Webb takes, which can capture these clusters, are produced by aiming the telescope at one area in the sky for hours at a time, so as to gradually reconstitute images of the faintest, farthest light in the universe.

Deep field shots often incorporate gravitational lensing, a phenomenon caused by objects so massive that they distort space and magnify the light coming from behind them, Furtak said. Gravitational lensing distorts the light seen in this photo of Abell 2744 and also creates a duplicative effect: the little red dot labeled [A,B,C] is the same object copied twice over.

Webb has been observing for five years, and could be out for around 20 more. Since its launch, the telescope has provided new insight into known phenomena and even introduced new phenomena entirely. “Each discovery opens new questions,” said Garcia Marin.

A new telescope, the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, will begin releasing photos on December 7. Compared to Webb, Roman will take photos of the universe with a larger field of view, much faster. The two telescopes will work in conjunction: after Roman surveys broad swaths of sky, Webb’s narrower cameras will be trained on smaller areas to capture greater detail.

It’s a “golden age” of astronomy, said Pontoppidan. Everyone—not just astronomers—is reaping the benefits.
